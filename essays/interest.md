## A Perpetual Taste Test

I began writing code in 2015, at age 11, starting with basic HTML and CSS to create local webpages. It quickly became clear to me that markup languages and visual design were not my forte, so I soon found myself learning scripting languages instead. The obvious starter was JavaScript, as I could incorporate it into my web development, but I never expected to stick with the language, use it as my favorite, or take it to server-side applications. I experimented with various other languages at this point, including PHP, SQL, C#, C++, Visual Basic, and batch scripting, but they ultimately ended up as mere useful tools in my arsenal, rather than career pathways or persistent hobbies. In late 2015, I joined Discord and quickly took an interest in the seemingly limitless capabilities of Discord bots, which could transform simple chat interactions on the front end into monstrous, system- and application-spanning procedures on the backend. Well, for me, it was that, or just implementing unusual features for a chatbot.

Throughout the next **five years**, I would maintain my focus on Discord bots and use my experience to develop them for applications I found useful in personal computing or online communities I participated in. The myriad open-source packages offered by npm made me feel like I could make a bot for everything I needed (or wanted) to do, even when a Discord integration was unnecessary for the task at hand. But while I could, I never stopped to think if I should.

The realization struck me in 2022–I felt as though I had not actually *made* anything. After years of using Node.js to develop programs for my transient interests in unlikely ways, I found myself more trained at reading library documentation than coding anything *new*, and the unrealized goal of making my own API wrapper only solidified my disappointment further. While I felt confident about my theoretical understanding of computers and programming languages, the practice I had to show for it was minimal—I had tasted every flavor, and maybe combined a few ingredients, but never truly cooked my *own* meal. Even as a new interest flourished (that of machine learning), the resulting code would, initially, extend only to a small project that unsuccessfully attempted to train AI to infer the Mandelbrot equation or a few of its patterns from point data. A few forays into low-level, object-oriented programming, along with a short episode of reverse engineering and game hacking later, my room for indecision would shrink to a critical point.  

## The Neural Shake-up

The year was 2022. Being nearly on my way out of High School, it was time to choose a field. What might have seemed like a difficult and inherently risky choice at the time, in hindsight, would turn out to be the most fruitful, but daunting, of all. OpenAI was beating the world's best esports players in tournaments with relatively small models and improving their early LLMs at exponential rates. Advancements in CNNs were making it clear that self-driving cars would be here within my lifetime. And most importantly to me, I had already learned the theoretical and practical basics of artificial neural networks, and I loved them. Any way I framed it, the future was AI, if not for the entire world, then at least for me. I was prepared to dedicate myself to the field regardless of its relevance or success, career opportunities or not.

Even as I started college and disappointedly slogged through countless credits of gen-ed and basic coding revision, as though I was being punished for my proactive youth, what was happening around me reinvigorated my hopes every step of the way. LLMs would go mainstream and effectively become *the* topic of our time, and the most pertinent issues with AI would inform me about the specific areas I wanted to fixate on. I promptly switched to the Data Science track for my CS degree and began concerning myself with AI optimization and safety.

## Getting on Board

Three years later, having worked through most of my general focus and CS requirements, it is time to turn with great interest to the matters of my chosen field. That is what I would have loved to say in the summer of 2026, before the state of things showed itself clearly. See, learning the fundamentals of neural networks is well and good, but your readiness for the industry will not pan out if that is all you do. That would be like trying to catch up to an Arleigh Burke-class destroyer on a rowboat. Upgrade to a dinghy with an outboard, and you may be able to hold on for a few seconds, but the 150-foot-tall vessel of technological progress will leave you behind eventually.

Getting on board thus requires you to either:
- have been on the ship since it left port,
- get an exclusive helicopter ride straight to the deck, or
- fire a grappling hook with perfect accuracy, and prepare for the g-forces of getting up to speed.

Born too late for the first option, and not well-connected enough for the second, it seemed I was left with the most uncomfortable of pathways to success. Like a stationary clutch facing a high-RPM flywheel, catching up would inevitably prove to be violent and leave me burnt out. But hold on, why are we all getting on the same boat in the first place?

## Just Getting Started

A fourth option, somewhat adjacent to the third, emerges just as the AI bandwagon fragments into innumerable sub-disciplines—get on your own boat. Either build it and sail out, buy it from someone else, or join a crew. Some people are training diffusion-based image models on specific concepts for clients, others are founding autonomous defense manufacturing startups, others are forming safety boards seeking to stop unchecked progress, and most are simply using the models available to the public for any number of creative (and often sloppy or wasteful) reasons. And they are doing all of this without necessarily being well-versed in all matters of AI. LLMs and agents might still be the cutting edge, but there is no longer one big *"AI"* in the room.

And so, here I am, excited for the future, yet fundamentally unsure which boat I'm getting on, if not building my own. As I take ICS 314 and revise JavaScript with a touch of TypeScript on top, I get to look back at my early choices, good and bad. While Software Engineering is not disjoint from my field of interest, it intersects in a comparatively small sliver of relevant skills when weighed against most other CS branches. More than that, however, it reminds me that I've made my choice, and that it will be a testament to my self-discipline and commitment to see it through.
