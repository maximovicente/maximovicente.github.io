## Out With the Age of the Ugly

The early internet, being before my time, is a space I cherish never having dealt with. Back when only a handful of routers connected the future, websites were enumerable, and most traffic was human, small-time developers inexplicably converged on some very questionable design choices, including neon text and scattered gifs. Archival examples offend even the radical web designers of today, and inform the rules and standards we now have for web layouts, color palettes, text fonts and sizing, spacing, and accessibility. 

The foundation of this newly standardized and human-friendly web is the UI framework. In many ways, UI frameworks are adjacent to coding standards in scripting languages, except that they systematize *visual* language elements rather than the source code invisible to end users. In this manner, UI frameworks, unlike code linters, are expected by *everyone*, not just developers. That explains the much more universal adoption of UI frameworks, as opposed to the yet-fragmented state of coding standards across languages.

## In With the Age of the Internet

The overall visual style of modern websites, imposed by the most prevalent UI frameworks and refined by web design's pioneers, has broader implications on our relationship with the internet and technology as a whole. Those implications are mostly positive, but occasionally negative. From the [nav-body-footer layout](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Learn_web_development/Howto/Design_and_accessibility/Common_web_layouts) emerges consistency—inexperienced web users or those of older age now have patterns to fall back on when deciphering the page rendered on their screen. The end result is that web users can now *get things done* on the internet more easily, effectively, and efficiently.

However, frameworks have to grapple with shifting technological paradigms, such as adaptability to new screen formats and computational devices. This applies to web and software development alike. Frameworks like Bootstrap include myriad classes to handle viewport scaling, but they are tailored to the set of devices expected of ordinary, connected people—a desktop PC, a phone, and maybe a tablet. Smartwatches and smart fridges, VR/XR goggles, and AR glasses now threaten existing frameworks with the need to adapt and expand to suit increasingly arbitrary screen shapes and aspects. If a framework is strictly suited to the three screen sizes that have dominated most of the internet's history, developers may struggle to ensure a reasonable layout on emergent outliers. Whether these outliers will bring entirely new layouts or leave frameworks unaffected is unclear. However, frameworks have certainly made viewport scaling easier, not harder, and updates that accommodate novel formats should continue that trend.

I know there is a bigger elephant to address: creativity. You may be tempted to treat practicality and creativity in web layouts as a tradeoff, a slider that cannot go both ways. To some extent, this is unavoidable, but a range exists in the middle where both are balanced to some minimum. Most websites, however, stay lamentably on the practical side of this range and sacrifice the visual storytelling potential of HTML and CSS. So, if developing a static website, with or without the help of a UI framework, I recommend starting with a well-thought-out, sufficiently unique mock-up of the site's design first that is based on *your* imagination, not just typical website layouts.

## Toward the Age of the CMS 

UI frameworks were born in a content-sparse internet, where updating and adding pages could reasonably entail editing and copying the underlying markup. When news outlets started to go online, and regular people began hosting their own blogs, demand emerged for a simpler way to update content, such that it also updates site-wide or wherever necessary. Template tags and cluttered _includes folders simply could not satisfy the need for sites to be more dynamic. Non-programmers needed a markup-free interface to add and edit posts or articles, and as users increasingly interacted with blogs or other online content, comment sections became the norm. Adding to that, ordinary people in non-tech professions started looking for ways to build their own website portfolios.

The Content Management System (CMS) is thus the natural evolution of website backends based on the internet's changing needs. When building a website, one can now skip HTML, CSS, JavaScript, and PHP entirely, as those are generated dynamically based on the values and properties of pre-defined content fields. These fields can also be organized into a more intuitive, responsive, and visually representative editor available directly through the browser. Block-based, drag-and-drop site building interfaces combine the best of UI frameworks with the best of dynamic content, and that makes them nearly flawless for the modern internet.

Nearly. Because the contemporary CMS is an expandable platform expected to support custom themes, plugins, and code snippets, it is subject to commercialization by the developers of these expansions, creating an entire industry. Open-source developers can publish these for free, but that also pushes commercial themes to provide a stripped-down, free version, while they offer the more niche, complex, and customizable features for a price. While unfortunate for the open-source community and low-budget web developers, if combined with the relative bloat of a CMS when content is sparse, this actually preserves a spot on the internet for static websites and CSS-based UI frameworks. That ultimately keeps HTML and CSS developers in business, and even more so when they start to specialize in developing CMS themes/plugins.

Will there be a replacement for the CMS? Unless the foundational languages underlying all websites change drastically to erode the need for Content Management Systems, I see no reason to replace them with anything new. For all intents and purposes, web development is right where it needs to be: small, simple websites can be built by HTML/CSS beginners with the help of UI frameworks and AI, while larger sites can leverage the dynamicism of a CMS to host countless pages and posts that are visually and functionally consistent. Lightweight CMSs and custom UI frameworks fill the gaps in between, making the internet more usable and familiar, far better than the visual free-for-all of the past.
